# WHAT ARE SMALL TREES CALLED?

Small trees are typically called "dwarf trees," "ornamental trees," or more specifically "saplings" when referring to young trees. The general classification for a small tree is one that grows up to 30 feet (about 9 meters) tall.[^1_1][^1_2][^1_3][^1_4]

### Common Terms for Small Trees

- "Sapling" refers to a young tree, especially one not over 10 centimeters (about four inches) in diameter.[^1_3]
- "Dwarf tree" describes a tree variety bred to remain smaller than its traditional species.[^1_5][^1_1]
- "Ornamental tree" is used for small trees chosen for visual appeal in landscaping.[^1_6][^1_7]
- Other synonyms include "plantlet," "seedling," "sprout," "scion," and "sprig".[^1_2]


### Examples of Small Trees

- Japanese Maple (often under 30 feet tall)
- Dwarf Alberta Spruce (usually under 10 feet tall)
- Royal Star Magnolia (around 12 feet tall)
- Gray Dogwood (about 9 feet tall)
- Dwarf Chestnut Oak (3–8 feet tall)[^1_7][^1_5]


### Usage Context

Small trees are popular for landscaping smaller yards, urban gardens, or for planting under power lines due to their modest height and spread.[^1_1][^1_7]

In summary, "sapling," "dwarf tree," "ornamental tree," and similar terms are used to describe small trees, especially those under 30 feet in height.[^1_2][^1_3][^1_1]

# terms for young trees and newly planted trees and plants

Young trees and newly planted trees and plants are commonly referred to by terms including "sapling," "seedling," and "transplant".[^2_5][^2_7][^2_9]

### Common Terms for Young Trees

- "Sapling": A young tree, usually under five years old and still in the early stages of growth.[^2_5]
- "Seedling": A plant that has just emerged from a seed and begun growing; this can refer to very young trees and other plants.[^2_7]
- "Transplant": A tree or plant that has been moved from one location and replanted elsewhere; typically refers to newly planted stock.[^2_9]
- "Plantlet": A small, newly grown plant, often used for young or juvenile specimens.[^2_3][^2_6]
- "Sprout": Can refer generally to new growth, including young shoots from seeds or roots.[^2_7]
- "Scion": A detached shoot or twig containing buds, used for grafting in horticulture.[^2_3]
- "Cutting": A piece removed from a mature plant for propagation and subsequent rooting.[^2_3]


### Terms for Newly Planted Trees and Plants

- "Balled and burlapped tree": Refers to transplant stock where the roots are wrapped for planting.[^2_9]
- "Bare-root stock": Young trees or plants sold without soil around the roots, typically for spring planting.[^2_9]

These terms are commonly used in horticulture, forestry, and garden contexts when discussing the growth stages or planting of tree and plant stock.[^2_3][^2_5]

# grafting in horticulture

Grafting in horticulture is a technique where tissues from two plants are joined so they grow together as a single plant. The part of the plant that provides the roots is called the "rootstock," while the upper part that is grafted on is called the "scion". This connection allows the desirable traits of each plant to be combined—for example, strong roots from one variety and high-quality fruit or flowers from another.[^3_1][^3_2][^3_3][^3_6]

### Key Facts about Grafting

- The most common reason for grafting is asexual propagation, especially for fruit trees and ornamental plants.[^3_1][^3_2][^3_3]
- For grafting to succeed, the vascular tissues (cambium layers) of the rootstock and scion must be closely aligned so they grow together and share water and nutrients.[^3_2][^3_3][^3_1]
- There are several types of grafting, including stem grafting and bud grafting. Bud grafting (inserting a bud rather than a whole twig) is common for fruit trees.[^3_3][^3_1][^3_2]
- Grafting can transmit disease resistance, control the size of fruit trees (dwarfing), improve hardiness, repair damaged trees, or produce multi-fruiting trees.[^3_2][^3_3]

Overall, grafting is a fundamental horticultural method that enables growers to combine the strengths of different plants for improved production, health, and adaptability.[^3_1][^3_3][^3_2]

# What are the main types of grafting techniques in horticulture

The main types of grafting techniques used in horticulture include several widely practiced methods, each suited to different plant species and situations.[^4_1][^4_2][^4_4][^4_6]

### Common Grafting Techniques

- Whip and Tongue Grafting: Both scion and rootstock are given matching slanted cuts and a 'tongue' incision to improve stability and cambium contact. It is especially popular for fruit trees and provides a high success rate.[^4_1][^4_2]
- Cleft Grafting: A split is made in the rootstock, and one or more wedge-shaped scions are inserted. This method is often used to change the cultivar of mature trees or repair damaged trees.[^4_2][^4_5]
- Bud (Budding) Grafting: A single bud from the desired plant is inserted under the bark of the rootstock. This is commonly used for propagating fruit trees and roses.[^4_6][^4_2]
- Approach Grafting: Two independent, self-sustaining plants are joined together until they fuse, after which one part is removed. It is used for difficult-to-graft species or plants growing in close proximity.[^4_2]
- Side-Veneer Grafting: The scion is inserted into a side cut in the rootstock stem; this is common for evergreens and ornamentals.[^4_1][^4_2]
- Bark (Rind) Grafting: Used on thicker rootstock when the bark is slipping. The scion is inserted under the bark of the stock, usually in spring.[^4_1]
- Splice (Whip) Grafting: A simple diagonal cut on both scion and rootstock, aligned and joined together; used for plants with flexible stems.[^4_9]
- Stub Grafting, Four-Flap (Banana) Grafting, and Awl Grafting: Specialized methods for certain species or advanced applications, such as pecans or rapid grafting.[^4_1]


### Special Cases

- Natural Grafting (Inosculation): Roots or branches of the same or compatible species naturally fuse if they contact and cambium layers align.[^4_2]

These techniques each have unique advantages and are selected based on the species, the size of the plant material, season, and horticultural objectives.[^4_1][^4_9][^4_2]

# scion

A scion is a detached living portion of a plant, such as a young shoot, twig, or bud, used in grafting to propagate plants with desirable characteristics. The scion is joined to the rootstock, which provides the root system, and together they grow as one plant. The resulting plant combines the traits of both the scion (such as fruit type or flower color) and the rootstock (such as disease resistance or size control).[^5_1][^5_2][^5_3][^5_4][^5_5]

Scions are typically selected from young, healthy growth, often taken during the dormant season, and used in various grafting methods, including whip-and-tongue, cleft, side, and bud grafting. The success of grafting depends on the close contact between the vascular tissues of the scion and rootstock.[^5_2][^5_1]

# scionic

The word "scionic" is not an established horticultural term, but it is sometimes used as an adjective relating to a "scion". In grafting, a "scion" is a detached shoot, twig, or bud from one plant that is joined to the rootstock of another plant, allowing the desirable characteristics of the scion (such as fruit type, flower color, or other features) to be preserved and propagated.[^6_1][^6_3][^6_6][^6_9]

If used, "scionic" would mean "pertaining to or characteristic of a scion," typically describing plant material or grafting practices involving the scion part of a grafted plant. However, this usage is rare and not standard in botanical or horticultural literature.[^6_3][^6_6][^6_1]

# Outline the steps to prepare scion wood for grafting

Preparing scion wood for grafting involves several important steps to ensure successful propagation and healthy growth.[^7_1][^7_2][^7_3]

### Steps to Prepare Scion Wood

1. **Select healthy wood:** Choose one-year-old woody growth from a vigorous, disease-free plant. Select sticks that are about the diameter of a pencil (0.25–0.5 inches), with healthy vegetative buds and without fruiting spurs.[^7_2][^7_1]
2. **Collect while dormant:** Cut scion wood during the plant’s dormant period, often in late winter or early spring, before buds swell.[^7_1][^7_2]
3. **Trim the ends:** Trim 1–2 cm (about an inch) off each end to remove any dead or damaged tissue. Cut the top so the scion has 3–4 viable buds.[^7_3]
4. **Shape for grafting:** Cut a long, straight, even wedge at the bottom of the scion to expose cambium, matching the typical technique (e.g., a wedge or slant depending on the graft method).[^7_4][^7_3]
5. **Maintain orientation:** Make sure the buds point upwards for proper physiological growth; do not invert the scion.[^7_3]
6. **Store cool and moist:** Immediately wrap the scion in a damp towel or paper, seal in a plastic bag, and refrigerate until you are ready to graft. Keep fruit out of the fridge, as ethylene gas can damage the scion.[^7_5][^7_1]

These steps help preserve the health and viability of scion wood, maximize graft compatibility, and ensure higher rates of successful union between scion and rootstock.[^7_2][^7_1][^7_3]

# graft etymology

The word "graft" in the horticultural sense—meaning "a shoot inserted into another plant"—originated in late 14th to 15th century Middle English as *graff* or *graffe*. This came from Old French *graife* or *greffe* (“grafting knife, carving tool; stylus, pen”) and ultimately from Latin *graphium* (“stylus”), which traces to Greek *grapheion* (“stylus”) from *graphein* (“to write”). The connection arises because the pencil-shaped shoot used in grafting resembles a stylus used for writing.[^8_1][^8_4]

Over time, "graft" also entered English in other senses: in British slang as "hard work" from the notion of digging, and in American slang as "corruption" or dishonest gain, likely from a figurative extension of the original botanical meaning or from the hard work/digging sense.[^8_2][^8_4][^8_1]

So, "graft" for plant propagation is etymologically linked to writing implements, carving, and digging tools, due to both the resemblance and the actions involved in both grafting and writing.[^8_4][^8_1]

# Explore the evolution of graft’s legal and political meanings

The term "graft" evolved distinct legal and political meanings in the 19th and 20th centuries, particularly in American English, where it became synonymous with political corruption—the unscrupulous use of official authority for personal gain. This development occurred as expanding governments and public works created opportunities for government officials and private interests to siphon public resources, often through kickbacks, bribery, and rigged contracts.[^9_1][^9_2][^9_3][^9_4][^9_5]

### Historical Development

- In its earliest legal and political context, "graft" referred specifically to the intentional misdirection of public funds or using public office for private benefit.[^9_1]
- The concept broadened to include not just direct theft or embezzlement, but also more sophisticated forms of corruption like influence peddling, insider trading using government information, and manipulation of public contracts for personal enrichment.[^9_1]
- Political machines in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, like New York's Tammany Hall, became infamous for large-scale graft, prompting public outcry and eventual reforms.[^9_6][^9_3]
- The phrase "honest graft," coined by George W. Plunkitt of Tammany Hall, described taking advantage of insider knowledge (e.g., purchasing land before a new public park was announced and reselling it at a profit), distinguishing it from more overt forms of corruption termed "dishonest graft".[^9_1]


### Legal and Political Usage

- Legally, graft is now recognized as a form of public corruption involving the abuse of entrusted power for private benefit, encompassing bribery, embezzlement, and other fraudulent acts.[^9_3][^9_1]
- In politics, it is seen as a threat to democratic institutions—diverting public funds away from intended purposes, distorting government priorities, and undermining public trust.[^9_4]
- The word's broader association with corruption and theft solidified in American political discourse after 1865, eventually becoming a blanket term for a wide range of dishonest practices involving public officials.[^9_2][^9_1]

The evolution of "graft" illustrates how language adapts to political realities, with the term coming to encapsulate both the mechanisms of corruption and society's shifting attitudes toward abuses of public trust.[^9_2][^9_3][^9_1]

# Compare graft’s etymology with the word grift and their relation

The words "graft" and "grift" have distinct etymologies but converged in meaning in American English to refer to various forms of corruption, often political or financial in nature.[^10_1][^10_2]

### Etymology of Graft

- "Graft" (horticulture, then corruption) comes from Middle English *graff* (“shoot or slip inserted in another plant”), borrowed from Old French *graife*, *greffe*, and Latin *graphium*, which ultimately traces to Greek *grapheion* (“writing implement”). Its original meaning references a shoot or twig and the act of inserting it, reminiscent of writing with a stylus.[^10_2][^10_3]
- The political meaning, as "corruption for personal gain," is an American development from the late 19th century, possibly influenced by the idea of something taken and inserted where it does not belong.[^10_2]


### Etymology of Grift

- "Grift" denotes swindling, petty theft, or cheating and is first attested in American English around the early 1900s.[^10_1]
- Its precise origin is uncertain, but likely comes from older slang "graft" (in its corruption sense), or possibly as a blend of "graft" and "gift" (in criminal cant, meaning swindle or deception).[^10_1]
- Some linguists believe "grift" developed by re-spelling "graft" to indicate a distinction between general, large-scale corruption ("graft") and petty criminal swindling ("grift").[^10_1]


### Relationship and Overlap

- Both terms ultimately came to signify dishonesty, illegality, and the abuse of trust for personal gain, although "graft" is more often associated with public or political corruption, while "grift" is used for confidence tricks, scams, or small cons.[^10_2][^10_1]
- In usage, "graft" refers to exploitation of official position, whereas "grift" suggests private swindling or scam artistry. Over time, they sometimes overlap when describing unethical or illegal financial activities.[^10_1]

In summary, while "graft" and "grift" originated differently—one from horticulture and writing, the other from cant or possibly influenced by "graft"—they now share a sense of dishonest gain, especially in American English, and are sometimes conflated because of their similar sounds and related meanings.[^10_2][^10_1]
